## LinkedIn Post

```
Most of my career has been this: not building new things, but keeping old things running.

That's why a number I came across recently stopped me in my tracks. Here's the thinking that's been on my mind:

→ The common take is "AI writes code now, so there's less work for developers." That assumes the total amount of work is fixed. After years inside large engineering orgs, I don't think that's how this plays out.

→ The number: an estimated 800 billion lines of legacy code sit in production worldwide. Modernizing it has historically cost ~$10-15 per line — too expensive for most of it to ever get touched. AI is dropping that toward ~$2 per line. The gap between those numbers is an addressable opportunity north of $1.6 trillion — not by replacing work, but by making decades of deferred work suddenly worth doing.

→ Every org has "that one system" — original team gone, docs thin, every change pushed to "next quarter" because the risk isn't worth it. That system is the norm, not the exception, and it's where a huge share of engineering time and stress already goes.

→ What changes for engineers: the valuable skill shifts from "write code fast" to "understand a messy legacy system and direct AI to rebuild it safely." Part archaeology, part risk management. The people who spent years on the systems everyone avoided suddenly have the skill set that matters most.

→ What changes for the industry: cheaper modernization doesn't shrink the market — it widens it, by clearing a backlog of projects that never cleared the bar before.
```
